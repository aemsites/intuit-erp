Accounts payable workflow: 5-step process to less lost invoices or payment delays

By Michael Kern

Key takeaways:

  • An accounts payable workflow is the structured process a business uses to manage and pay its invoices from vendors and suppliers.
  • The workflow typically involves receiving, coding, matching, approving, scheduling, paying, and recording invoices and related documentation.
  • A well-defined accounts payable workflow improves accuracy, efficiency, vendor relations, and cash flow management while also reducing risks.
  • Common challenges include manual data entry, lost invoices, approval bottlenecks, and a lack of visibility into the overall process.

Managing finances effectively is crucial for any business, and a significant part of that is handling accounts payable. The accounts payable workflow, specifically, is the backbone of how companies pay their bills.

Yet, according to QuickBooks' Business Solutions Survey, 95% of respondents report challenges with their current digital business solutions.

This widespread dissatisfaction highlights the need for streamlined, efficient processes. Without a well-defined accounts payable workflow, you might face late payments or strained vendor relationships.

This article will delve into every aspect of the accounts payable workflow, from its basic steps to optimization strategies.

How does the accounts payable workflow work?

An accounts payable workflow is the series of steps a business follows from the moment it receives an invoice to the moment the invoice is paid and recorded.

It's a structured approach designed to ensure that all invoices are legitimate, accurate, and timely.

This process involves careful verification, approval routing, and secure payment methods. It moves the process from invoice receipt to payment.

Who manages an accounts payable workflow?

The accounts payable department, often a part of the larger finance or accounting team, typically manages this workflow. In smaller businesses, it might be a single individual, such as an office manager or bookkeeper.

Larger organizations might divide the responsibilities among several roles:

  • Accounts payable clerk: Handles day-to-day tasks like data entry and invoice processing.
  • Accounts payable specialist: Focuses on more complex tasks, such as resolving discrepancies and managing vendor relationships.
  • Accounts payable manager: Oversees the entire department and ensures compliance with company policies.
  • CFO or controller: Provides high-level oversight and strategic direction for the finance function.

Common documents in the accounts payable workflow

Several key documents are integral to a smooth accounts payable process:

  • Purchase order (PO): A document the seller issues to the buyer authorizing a purchase.
  • Invoice: A bill from the seller to the buyer detailing the goods or services provided and the amount due.
  • Receiving report: A document confirming that the customer has received the goods or the business has rendered the services.
  • Payment voucher: An internal document authorizing payment to a vendor.
  • Check or electronic payment confirmation: Proof of payment to a company.

7 steps in the accounts payable workflow

While seemingly straightforward, the accounts payable workflow is a multi-stage process that requires careful attention to detail. It's more than just paying bills; it's about ensuring accuracy, maintaining vendor relationships, and controlling your company's cash outflow.

Breaking it down into these seven distinct steps helps visualize the entire process and highlight areas for improvement.

1. Invoice receipt and intake

The workflow begins the moment an invoice arrives. This could be through various channels—a physical invoice in the mail, an emailed PDF, or even a submission through an online vendor portal.

The key here is having a systematic way to receive and initially process all incoming invoices, regardless of their format. A designated email address (e.g., ap@yourcompany.com) or a central physical inbox will help prevent invoices from getting lost or overlooked.

2. Invoice data capture and coding

Once received, the crucial information from the invoice needs to be extracted and entered into your accounting system. This includes data points like:

  • Vendor name and ID: Ensuring you're paying the correct entity.
  • Invoice number: A unique identifier for tracking.
  • Invoice date: Important for payment terms and aging reports.
  • Due date: Crucial for timely payment and avoiding late fees.
  • Amount due: The total amount to be paid.
  • Line item details: Descriptions of goods or services, quantities, and unit prices.
  • General ledger (GL) codes: Assigning the expense to the correct accounting category.

Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technology can scan invoices and automatically extract this data, significantly speeding up the process and improving accuracy.

3. Invoice matching (three-way matching)

This is arguably the most critical step for ensuring accuracy and preventing fraud. Three-way matching involves comparing the invoice to two other key documents:

  • The purchase order (PO): Did we authorize this purchase? Does the invoice match the agreed-upon price and quantity?
  • The receiving report: Did we actually receive the goods or services in the quantity and condition we expected?

Verifying these three documents against each other will help you identify any discrepancies before payment is made.

4. Invoice approval routing

Once the invoice has been matched and verified, it needs to be approved for payment. The approval process will vary depending on your company's internal controls and the size of the invoice.

Smaller invoices might be approved by a single AP clerk, while larger amounts may require multiple levels of authorization, perhaps from a department head, manager, or even the CFO.

Automated workflow tools can route invoices to the correct approvers based on pre-set rules, sending reminders, and escalating overdue approvals.

5. Payment scheduling

After approval, the invoice is ready to be scheduled for payment. This involves considering the vendor's payment terms (e.g., Net 30, Net 60, Due Upon Receipt) and your company's cash flow situation. Strategically scheduling payments allows you to maximize early payment discounts (if available) while maintaining sufficient cash on hand.

6. Payment execution

This is the actual disbursement of funds to the vendor. Common payment methods include:

  • Checks: Traditional, but becoming less common due to processing time and security concerns.
  • Automated Clearing House (ACH) transfers: Electronic payments directly from your bank account to the vendor's.
  • Wire transfers: Faster but often involve higher fees.
  • Virtual cards: Single-use digital cards that offer enhanced security and control.
  • Credit cards: Using a business credit card.

The best method will depend on the vendor's preferences, the payment amount, and your company's policies.

7. Record keeping and reconciliation

The final step is meticulous recordkeeping. All documentation related to the invoice—the invoice itself, the PO, the receiving report, proof of payment, and any communication with the vendor—should be securely stored and easily accessible. This leads to more efficient:

  • Audits: Provide a clear audit trail for internal and external auditors.
  • Reporting: Generate accurate financial reports.
  • Vendor inquiries: Quickly resolve any questions or disputes that may arise.
  • Reconciliation: Balance the accounts payable ledger with payments made.

Benefits of an accounts payable workflow

Implementing a structured accounts payable workflow isn't just about following a set of procedures; it's a strategic move that can positively impact various aspects of your business. It moves beyond simply paying invoices and provides broader advantages.

An efficient accounts payable workflow contributes significantly to overall financial health, efficiency, and vendor relationships. Here are the key benefits to consider:

  • Enhanced accuracy: Reduces errors like incorrect data entry, duplicate payments, and missed invoices through checks and balances.
  • Improved efficiency: Automates tasks, freeing up the AP team for more strategic activities.
  • Stronger vendor relationships: Ensures timely payments, fostering trust and potentially leading to better terms.
  • Optimized cash flow: Provides better visibility into payment obligations for improved forecasting and planning.
  • Reduced risk: Minimizes fraud and compliance issues through implemented controls and documentation.
  • Better cost control: Prevents overpayments and late fees and facilitates spending analysis.
  • Improved financial visibility: Provides a clear audit trail and data for accurate reporting and decision-making.

Common challenges in the accounts payable workflow

While a well-designed accounts payable workflow offers numerous benefits, the reality is that many businesses struggle with recurring challenges. These aren't just minor inconveniences; they can significantly impact efficiency, accuracy, and even vendor relationships.

Know the common pain points so you can find effective solutions.

Manual data entry

One of the most pervasive challenges is the continued reliance on manual data entry. It's a task that's both inherently prone to human error and time-consuming, with businesses spending as much as 25 hours per week on manual data entry, according to our Business Solutions Survey.

Think about it: transposing numbers, misreading handwriting, or simply overlooking a crucial detail on a lengthy invoice—these mistakes happen easily.

The consequences, however, can range from incorrect payments to strained relationships with valued suppliers, not to mention the time spent correcting those errors.

The QuickBooks Business Solutions Survey revealed that 85% of business owners think data entry and reconciliation hurts growth.

Missing invoices

Invoices can arrive from various sources, like email, physical mail, and online portals. Without a streamlined, centralized system for intake, it's surprisingly easy for an invoice to get lost in the shuffle. A misplaced paper invoice or an overlooked email can lead to late payment fees, missed opportunities for early payment discounts, and a headache when it comes time to reconcile accounts.

The seemingly simple act of properly receiving the invoice is often a major point of failure.

40% of respondents in a recent QuickBooks Survey of business owners said that keeping track of documents is one of the most challenging aspects of doing taxes.

Approval bottlenecks

Even if an invoice is received and entered correctly, it can still get stuck in the approval process. This often happens when approval workflows are poorly defined, overly complicated, or simply not followed.

Imagine an invoice sitting on someone's desk (or in their inbox) for days or weeks while the payment due date looms closer. Chasing down approvers and managing exceptions becomes a full-time job in itself, pulling AP staff away from other critical tasks.

Inconsistent information

Three-way matching—comparing the invoice, purchase order, and receiving report—is essential for accuracy. But what happens when those three documents don't agree?

Discrepancies in quantity, price, or even item description can derail the entire process. Resolving these issues often requires manual investigation, back-and-forth communication with vendors, and a significant time investment.

Lack of visibility

Without a clear, real-time view of the entire accounts payable process, it's like flying blind. You can't easily track where invoices are in the workflow, identify bottlenecks, or monitor key performance indicators.

This lack of visibility makes it difficult to manage cash flow effectively, proactively address potential problems, and make informed decisions about your spending.

Too many apps

Many businesses today suffer from "app overload" because they're using a multitude of separate software applications for different aspects of their operations.

This can be particularly problematic for the accounts payable workflow.

You might have one app for invoice scanning, another for approvals, a separate accounting system, and yet another for payment processing. This fragmented approach leads to:

  • Data silos: Information is scattered across different systems, making it difficult to get a complete picture of your AP process.
  • Manual data transfers: Data often needs to be manually exported and imported between apps, increasing the risk of errors and wasting valuable time.
  • Integration headaches: Getting different apps to "talk" to each other can be a complex and costly undertaking.
  • Increased costs: Paying for multiple software subscriptions can add up quickly.
  • Unused software: You may have unused subscriptions that you forgot about.
