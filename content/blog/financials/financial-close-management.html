What are the primary drivers of intercompany friction in the financial close?

Manual financial close management is insufficient to oversee inter-entity activity, especially during phases of growth. Every new subsidiary brings numerous new departments and transactions that financial teams must reconcile, and they quickly hit productivity ceilings.

At some point, manual oversight becomes mathematically impossible and creates an anchor that drags down both decision velocity and audit readiness.

Here are the core drivers of friction that multi-entity companies experience during financial close, and how to address them:

1. Exponential growth due to transaction volume

All transactions from all departments must pass through the financial department at some point. This means that adding just one or two new entities can lead to an exponential increase in intercompany transactions. With the industry already seeing a 23% year-over-year rise in transaction volumes, accounting for manufacturing operations quickly becomes unmanageable.

The sheer volume of transactions inevitably lets things fall through the cracks. Discrepancies may only surface in the final hours of close. Resolving them takes time that companies may not have, putting them at risk of compliance and reporting infractions.

2. Timing gaps and disparate charts of accounts across subsidiaries

Manual processes that rely heavily on disparate charts of accounts create a high risk of discrepancies. Worse, with CFO.com reporting that 50% of finance teams still take over a week to close, there’s less time to spot and resolve discrepancies.

The problem is aggravated when intercompany transactions occur at the end of the month. The first entity could record a transfer on the 30th, but the recipient entity may not log it until the 2nd. This creates a single transaction split across two month-end statements, which were generated weeks apart.

Such inconsistent data tracking forces senior accounting talent into “forensic data cleaning” rather than high-level oversight, with some teams spending “more time trying to explain the mismatches than actually fixing them,” CFO.com adds.

3. The reconciliation trap and resulting reporting lag

Manual consolidation is a linear solution to an exponential problem. Multi-entity transactions multiply, but accounting team capacity is fixed. There’s no reasonable way to scale, and newly onboarded team members operate at as low as 25% productivity and don’t rise to the level of existing employees for one to two years.

Without the tools to compile and consolidate reports quickly, data grows stale. If your team takes more than 15 days to reconcile transactions and generate a consolidated report, any management decisions you make could be misinformed. Old data obscures your present, real-time financial standing and hampers decision-making.

4. Decentralized data silos and file-based workflows

Inadequate reporting and analysis are already a significant concern affecting 45% of business leaders. Decentralization only weakens transparency further. With no central control or automated reporting, finance teams must manually stitch together plant-based or subsidiary exports into a single report.

These disconnected silos prevent a global view of the company’s financial posture. For CFOs, this means having limited insights on cash flow and working capital until the close is finalized, essentially flying blind for weeks on end.

5. Regulatory and tax-nexus complexity in multi-jurisdictional operations

Expanding into new jurisdictions brings new and complicated tax considerations and reporting requirements. As reported by the Tax Foundation, companies spent an average of $10.2 million on outside tax assistance, with 49% of that directed toward state and federal compliance.

With over 700,000 regulatory changes reported across industries in 2023 alone, manual intercompany transfers increase the risk of noncompliance and inadvertent infractions.

Without a centralized, system-generated audit trail for every transfer, multi-entity businesses expose themselves to significant liability during regulatory reviews.
