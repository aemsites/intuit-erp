How does business efficiency work?

Business efficiency is the velocity at which an enterprise converts working capital, labor, and assets into net margin and cash flow. For multi-entity firms, this is measured by the precision with which operational spend can be linked to financial outcomes in real time.

Put more simply, it's the relationship between what you put into the business and what you get back.

For multi-entity firms, efficiency is harder to measure and harder to manage. Every entity may run different cost structures, reporting calendars, and allocation methods. That fragmentation makes it difficult to see at group level where employee productivity is strong, where spend is being wasted, and whether capital is allocated where it generates the strongest return. High-efficiency multi-entity firms solve this by consolidating visibility at group level so capital, headcount, and operating spend can be compared and redirected on a common basis.

It's also worth distinguishing efficiency from efficacy. Efficacy refers to a company's ability to fulfill its obligations to clients and customers. A business can run lean internal processes and still fall short on its contracts or service commitments. Both matter, but the distinction is important. One points to how you operate, the other to whether you deliver.

What are the 5 best KPIs for measuring enterprise-grade efficiency?

KPIs like gross margin and revenue growth are useful, yet they tell you little about efficiency. The ones that matter most show you how to improve business efficiency by connecting operational activity to financial outcomes as you have the data you need to identify where performance is slipping and why.

The following five KPIs are worth prioritizing:

1. Time to close

Every additional day in the close cycle means the data behind leadership decisions is further removed from current trading conditions. The quicker the turnaround, especially in multi-entity firms, the quicker leaders act to course correct when a KPI moves in the wrong direction.

Gartner predicts that finance teams using AI-enabled cloud ERP will close their books 30% faster than peers by 2028. It's a clear example of how ERP can improve a business's efficiency at scale.

Transitioning to a continuous close shifts reconciliations from a month-end sprint to a daily, automated workflow. Intuit Enterprise Suite enables this real-time oversight across every entity, flagging timing mismatches and performance issues as they occur rather than weeks after the fact. By resolving variances throughout the period, you widen the window for course correction and ensure your consolidated books remain audit-ready at all times.

This visibility turns KPIs from a reporting tool into an early warning system, enabling problems to be resolved in the same period they emerge.

2. Intercompany transaction efficiency

Fragmented systems and manual consolidations traditionally slow down the delivery of financial insights and increase overall operational risk. Part of that is time spent collecting and validating data, leaving finance teams with just 31% of their time for high-value activities like strategic analysis.

Across multiple entities, manual work increases by the time balances like intercompany transactions are reconciled. Having an accurate picture of cash and intercompany balances across your portfolio entities provides the clarity needed to steer decision-making without waiting for period-end consolidation.

That's the level of visibility that lets you spot a cash shortfall at the individual-entity level and address it by redirecting surplus cash or planning a credit draw in advance. These are the kinds of ERP software benefits that scale with every entity you add.

3. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)

Disconnected tools create workflow friction, and license costs stack up across a multi-entity portfolio. According to Zylo's 2025 SaaS Management Index, organizations don’t use 52.7% of the SaaS licenses they purchase, meaning a significant share of software spend delivers no operational return.

Consolidating into a unified platform removes that dead weight and gives finance leaders a single view of costs and supplier relationships across every entity. Fewer vendors also means less time spent on contract cycles, renewal negotiations, and vendor management.

For example, ERP modernization that replaces legacy systems and associated payroll and finance software with an all-in-one suite cuts software costs and makes the remaining spend easier to justify at board level. You can also take the lead in making the same case across other departments, turning software consolidation into a group-wide cost control initiative.

4. Labor costs

When finance stops having to manually code transactions, reconcile intercompany balances, and rebuild project budgets from scratch, there’s more time to run valuable tasks like margin analysis and forecast accuracy. According to Tipalti's 2025 Global Finance Outlook, teams currently lose 11 hours per week to manual tasks, nearly 72 workdays per year.

Automate the high-volume, low-judgment work across your entities, and the labor cost saving goes straight to the bottom line. This frees senior finance talent from tedious, repetitive processing tasks, allowing them to focus on strategic priorities and high-impact work.

The labor cost savings from automating high-volume work across entities go directly to margin and set a template for the same conversation across the wider business.

5. Revenue leakage

Data silos are the primary cause of revenue leakage. According to the DATAVERSITY Trends in Data Management survey, 68% of organizations cite data silos as their top concern. In practice, this shows up as outdated pricing that fails to reflect rising input costs, unbilled expenses, and contract margin erosion, which remain hidden until the end of the quarter.

Efficiency in 2026 means identifying these leaks in real time. With a consolidated view across all entities, you can use AI-driven insights to reprice underperforming contracts, recapture unbilled costs, and redirect investment to the work that delivers the greatest margins. This proactive protection of existing revenue is a more precise measure of efficiency than top-line growth alone.
